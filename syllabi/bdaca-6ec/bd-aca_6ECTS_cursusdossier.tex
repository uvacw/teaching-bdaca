\chapter{Planning of testing and teaching}

The course consists of seven teaching weeks with two meetings per week, followed by an assessment period. In the first weekly meeting, the instructor gives short lectures on key concepts and methods, followed by seminar-style discussions of theoretical considerations, methodological choices, and examples from the literature. In the second weekly meeting, students work in practical lab sessions in which they apply the techniques they have learned to course materials or their own project data.

During lab sessions, collaboration and mutual support are strongly encouraged. Students are encouraged to discuss programming concepts, compare approaches, ask questions, and help one another work through exercises. This collaboration supports learning but does not permit students to share or jointly produce code, analyses, or written material for individual assessments.

The course contains three individual summative assessments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item an in-class coding exercise in Week 4 (25\%);
    \item an individual project pitch in Week 7 (10\%);
    \item a final individual project (65\%).
\end{itemize}

The Week 4 coding exercise assesses students' ability to independently read, adapt, test, debug, and explain Python code. The Week 7 project pitch assesses the feasibility and methodological justification of the proposed research plan. It also assesses students' ability to identify and explain a relevant limitation, risk, or methodological weakness in their proposed study and to propose a feasible improvement. This provides individual summative evidence for ILO H. The pitch further enables the lecturer to assess students' understanding of their proposed data, methods, and analytical choices verbally. During the Week 7 workshop, students are encouraged to provide constructive feedback to peers. This peer-feedback activity is formative and is not graded.

\section*{Generative-AI safeguards}

The in-class coding exercise is completed individually without the use of generative-AI tools, subject to the applicable examination arrangements. In other course work, generative AI may be used only to clarify programming concepts or debug small, de-identified code snippets or toy examples. Students may not upload course datasets, substantial project data, full analysis notebooks, or sensitive or personally identifiable data to external AI services. They may not use generative AI as a black-box analysis engine for graded work.

Students remain responsible for the correctness, efficiency, reproducibility, and documentation of all submitted code. Non-trivial generative-AI contributions to code must be disclosed in the relevant notebook or script.

\chapter{History of the course}

In response to the feedback by the test review committee in 2019, the following changes were applied:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Emphasised in the section ``Testable Objectives'' that---in contrast to the 12 ECTS course---students should be able to demonstrate, reflect, and apply knowledge about \emph{fundamental}, rather than \emph{advanced}, techniques of automated content analysis. Likewise, testing is based on the extent to which students demonstrate knowledge and skills at the level of \emph{fundamental} techniques. This distinguishes the 6 ECTS course from more advanced courses in Big Data and Automated Content Analysis.
\end{itemize}

In 2022, the course received a thorough update following the publication of the new textbook \citep{cssbook}.

In 2023, the course was updated to incorporate recent developments in machine learning. The course continues to focus on fundamental techniques of automated content analysis; Week 7 includes an outlook on state-of-the-art topic-modelling and machine-learning approaches.

In 2024, the course was updated in response to developments in AI-assisted coding. The take-home exam was redesigned to focus more strongly on debugging and explaining existing code, including a short video walkthrough. In addition, a final in-class project pitch was introduced to enable students to exchange ideas and provide feedback while there was still time to revise their projects.

In 2025, these changes were maintained and consolidated. The take-home examination continued to require a walkthrough video, and the in-class project presentation was formally integrated into the course structure.

In 2026, the course was revised in response to feedback from the Toetscommissie following its review of the RM Year 2 curriculum. The Toetscommissie noted that it was not sufficiently clear how ILO H, concerning academic attitudes, was summatively assessed. It also recommended making safeguards concerning generative-AI use more explicit and suggested considering small individual in-class graded assignments.

In response, the 2026--2027 edition introduces an individual in-class coding exercise in Week 4, worth 25\% of the final grade. The exercise requires students to independently read, adapt, test, debug, and explain Python-based data-analysis workflows. It provides direct evidence of individual programming competence and understanding without reliance on generative-AI tools.

ILO H has been reformulated to focus on students' critical reflection on the strengths, limitations, and potential improvements of their own planned research. This outcome is summatively assessed through the individual Week 7 project pitch, worth 10\% of the final grade. The pitch rubric includes a criterion requiring students to identify and explain a relevant limitation, risk, or methodological weakness in their proposed study and to propose a feasible improvement. The pitch also enables the lecturer to assess students' understanding of their proposed data, methods, and analytical choices verbally. This provides an additional safeguard for assessment validity in the context of AI-assisted coding, because students must be able to explain and justify their own work.

The course file now includes an explicit policy on generative-AI use. Generative-AI tools are not permitted during the in-class coding exercise. For other course work, students may use such tools only within defined limits; they may not upload course datasets or full analysis notebooks to external services, use AI as a black-box analysis engine, or submit non-trivial AI-assisted code without disclosure. Collaboration and mutual support are strongly encouraged during lab sessions. Peer feedback in the Week 7 project workshop is formative and is not graded.
