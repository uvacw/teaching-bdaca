\section*{Week 3: Data wrangling and exploratory data analysis}

Of course, you don't need Python to do statistics. Whether it's R, Stata, or SPSS -- you probably already have a tool that you are comfortable with. But you also do not want to switch to a different environment just for getting a correlation. And you definitly don't want to do advanced data wrangling in SPSS\ldots
This week, we will discuss different ways of organizing your data (e.g., long vs wide formats) as well as how to do conventional statistical tests and simple plots in Python.

\subsection*{Monday, 10--11. Lecture.}
\textsc{\ding{52} Chapter 6: Data wrangling}\\
\textsc{\ding{52} Chapter 7.1. Simple exploratory data analysis}\\
\textsc{\ding{52} Chapter 7.2. Visualizing data}\\

We will learn how to get your data in the right shape and how to get a first understanding of your data, using exploratory analysis and visualization techniques. We will cover data wrangling with pandas: converting between wide and long formats (melting and pivoting), aggregating data, joining datasets, and so on.

\subsection*{Thursday, 13--11. Lab session and short lecture.}
We will apply the techniques discussed during the previous lectures to multiple datasets. In addition, we will start exploring the topic of next week: processing textual data. This short lecture will introduce key ideas about how text can be represented and analyzed using computational methods. We will discuss different approaches to representing textual data (such as tokenization and Bag-of-Words models) and experiment with some simple examples in Python.

\section*{Week 4: Processing textual data}

In this week, we will dive into how to work with textual data. How is text represented, how can we process it, and how can we extract useful information from it? 
Because text as written by humans is often quite messy, we will explore ways to represent it in a cleaner and more structured way. We will introduce the Bag-of-Words (BOW) representation and demonstrate several ways of transforming text into matrices that can be analyzed quantitatively.

\subsection*{Monday, 17--11. Short lecture and lab session.}

\textsc{\ding{52} Chapter 9: Processing text}\\
\textsc{\ding{52} Chapter 10: Text as data}\\
\textsc{\ding{52} Chapter 11, Sections 11.1--11.3: Automatic analysis of text}\\

Additional recommended background reading on stopwords: \cite{Nothman2018}.

This session combines a short lecture with a hands-on exercise. We will further explore techniques such as lemmatization, stopword removal, n-grams, word counts and word co-occurrences, and regular expressions. Building on Thursday’s introduction, we will implement these methods in Python and continue developing our first automated content analysis script, combining our knowledge of data structures and programming concepts with introductory text-processing techniques.

\section*{Week 5: Unsupervised approaches to text analysis}

In this week, we will make the transition from traditional statistical models to modern unsupervised machine learning techniques for text analysis. We will discuss how these approaches relate to earlier methods such as topic modeling and how recent advances in transformer-based language models have reshaped exploratory text analysis.

\subsection*{Monday, 24--11. Lecture: From topic models to embeddings.}

\textsc{\ding{52} Chapter 11.5: Unsupervised text analysis — Topic modeling and beyond}\\
\textsc{\ding{52} \cite{kroon_advancing_2024}}\\
\textsc{\ding{52} \cite{Maier2018a}}\\

In this lecture, we will examine the logic of unsupervised text modeling and contrast traditional approaches such as LDA with modern embedding-based techniques. We will introduce the concept of BERTopic as an example of how transformer models can uncover themes in large text collections without manual coding.

\subsection*{Thursday, 27--11. Lab session}

In this lab session, we will apply BERTopic to textual data, experimenting with how clustering emerges from contextual embeddings. We will compare its output to simpler unsupervised approaches and discuss strengths, limitations, and interpretability. We will also provide guidance on evaluating unsupervised models and integrating them into computational communication research.
